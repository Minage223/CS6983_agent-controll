**Statement of Interest — PhD in Human-Computer Interaction, Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

In my machine learning course, my team fine-tuned a transformer to classify sentiment in Reddit mental-health support posts. On paper it worked: macro-F1 of 0.81, compared with 0.72 for a TF-IDF baseline. But when I did the error analysis, I found a pattern the score hid. The model kept labeling sarcastic and dark-humor posts as negative. People in support communities often use humor to talk about hard things, and some of those posts were the writer coping, connecting with others, or making light of something painful. The model read those posts as distress. I want to study that gap between how people actually talk about their struggles online and how computational systems interpret what they say.

**Research experience**

Since October 2025, I've been an undergraduate research assistant in the Social Computing Lab at Brookfield University with Prof. A. Moreno. I work on an interview study led by PhD student J. Kim about how teens aged 15–17 use online communities for mental-health support. My role was supporting, not leading, and it gave me close contact with the data at every stage. I sent recruitment messages to community moderators and ran the eligibility screener. I transcribed and de-identified 14 of the 16 interviews, which meant replacing names, schools, and server names with placeholders while keeping the meaning of what participants said. I then coded 6 transcripts with the team's codebook. In our inter-rater reliability check with J. Kim, we reached a Cohen's kappa of 0.71 after one round of reconciliation. The paper is now under review, and I'm listed in the acknowledgments.

The coding taught me the most. Our disagreements were rarely careless mistakes. They usually came from passages where a teen's tone was ambiguous: a joke that might have been a disclosure, or a complaint about a community that was also a sign of belonging to it. Resolving those passages took discussion and context that a single label couldn't capture. It was the same problem I had seen in the classifier, this time from the human side.

**Research interests**

During a PhD, I want to combine qualitative and computational methods to understand how young people express distress, humor, and support in online communities, and how automated systems such as moderation tools, content classifiers, and recommendation systems read and respond to that expression. Questions I'm curious about include:

- When do automated systems misread coping or community humor as risk, and what happens to teens and moderators when they do?
- How can qualitative insights, like the ambiguities we argued over while coding, shape how these systems are designed and evaluated?
- How do we study this kind of data responsibly, given that it comes from minors and is sensitive by nature?

I don't have a fixed project yet, and I expect these questions to change through coursework and work with an advisor. I'm drawn to them because they draw on both kinds of work I've done so far: carefully reading what people say, and building systems that try to do the same thing at scale.
