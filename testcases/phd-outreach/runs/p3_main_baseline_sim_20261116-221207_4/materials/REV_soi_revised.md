**Statement of Interest — PhD in Human-Computer Interaction, Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

Last winter I transcribed interviews with teens about how they use online communities when they're struggling. Most of that work was slow and careful: listening, correcting the auto-transcript, and swapping names and schools for placeholders. But one interview has stayed with me. A teen described a Discord server that was the only place she could admit she was having a bad day. Then the moderators added a bot that automatically deleted posts with certain words, and some of hers just disappeared. [*If J. Kim approves: She called it "being shushed by a robot."*]

I get why the mods did it. They're volunteers trying to keep people safe, and a word filter is one of the few tools they have. But I keep wondering who those tools are really for. Are they protecting the teens who are posting, or mainly the people responsible for the space? And if your posts keep disappearing, what does that teach you about what you're allowed to say when you need help? That question is the main reason I'm applying.

I should be clear about how much research I've actually done. Since October 2025 I've been an undergraduate research assistant in Prof. A. I haven't designed or led a study. But being that close to the data is how I ended up with a question of my own, and it's why I want to learn to do the rest.

Coding was the part that surprised me. I expected disagreements to come from mistakes, but most of them came from tone: whether a joke was also a way of saying something hard. My favorite part was the error analysis, because the model kept marking sarcastic or dark-humor posts as negative. Those are often the posts where someone is coping. A word filter is far simpler than that model, but I think it has the same problem: it reacts to the words people use, not to what they mean.

I don't have a research question yet so much as things I keep wondering about. How do teens notice and react when their posts are filtered? Do they rephrase, go quiet, or leave? How do moderators decide where to draw the line? And could these tools be built with the people asking for help in mind, not just the people managing risk? I'd want to start by talking to teens and moderators, but I know I have a lot to learn about how to study this well, especially with young people.
