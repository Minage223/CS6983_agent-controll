**Statement of Interest — PhD in Human-Computer Interaction, Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

One of the teens in an interview study I worked on told us about an online server that was the only place she felt she could admit she was having a bad day. Then the moderators added a bot that automatically deleted posts containing certain words, and some of her posts disappeared. [*If approved: She described it as "being shushed by a robot."*] I understand why the moderators did it. They were volunteers trying to keep a vulnerable community safe, and automated filters are one of the few tools that scale. But her account has stayed with me, and so has a question it raised: when moderation tools are built to manage risk, who are they actually serving, and what do they teach young people about what is okay to say when they need help?

I want to pursue a PhD in HCI to study how automated moderation shapes the way teens seek support online. I'm especially interested in whether these tools change what teens feel able to disclose, and how they might be designed with the people asking for help in mind.

**Research experience**

Since October 2025, I've been an undergraduate research assistant in the Social Computing Lab at Brookfield University, working with Prof. A. Moreno on an interview study led by PhD student J. Kim. The study looks at how teens aged 15–17 use online communities for mental-health support. My role was supporting, not leading, and it kept me close to the data at every stage. I sent recruitment messages to community moderators and ran the eligibility screener. I transcribed and de-identified 14 of the 16 interviews, replacing names, schools, and server names with placeholders while keeping the meaning of what participants said. I then coded 6 transcripts with the team's codebook. In our inter-rater reliability check with J. Kim, we reached a Cohen's kappa of 0.71 after one round of reconciliation. The paper is under review, and I'm listed in the acknowledgments.

Transcribing taught me to listen closely to how teens talk about hard things. Coding showed me how often that talk is ambiguous. Most of our disagreements were about tone: whether a joke was also a disclosure, or whether a complaint about a community was also a sign of belonging to it. We could only resolve those passages through discussion and context.

**Computational background**

I saw the same ambiguity from the other side in my machine learning course. My team fine-tuned a transformer to classify sentiment in Reddit mental-health support posts and reached a macro-F1 of 0.81, compared with 0.72 for a TF-IDF baseline. I led the error analysis and found that the model consistently labeled sarcastic and dark-humor posts as negative. Those are the posts where people are often coping, and where coders like me had to slow down and think. A word filter is much simpler than that model, but it faces the same problem: it reacts to the words people use, not to what they mean or need.

I'm finishing a B.S. in Computer Science with a minor in Data Science, with coursework in HCI and social computing. Last summer, as a Data Analyst Intern at Brightpath Learning, I built a Tableau dashboard of student engagement and the SQL behind it. Part of that work was fixing double-counted sessions by redefining what counts as a session. It taught me how much a single definition built into a system shapes what an organization believes about the people using it.
