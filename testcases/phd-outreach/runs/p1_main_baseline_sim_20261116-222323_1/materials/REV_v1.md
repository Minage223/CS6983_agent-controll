# Statement of Purpose: PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences

In my first semester as a research assistant, I was building features from anonymized mobility data: phone location pings, aggregated to neighborhoods, used to forecast bus ridership. At some point I looked up how the vendor collected the data. It came from apps that asked users once for permission to share their location. I remember thinking: *I doubt anyone tapping "allow" pictured this.*

I kept working on the project, and it was good work. But that thought stayed with me more than anything about the model. I've realized it's a question I want to spend years on. Not how to make the forecast better, but what people understand about the systems that use their data, and what the people using those systems understand about them.

I came to this through numbers. I studied economics, then worked for a year as a data analyst at a regional transit agency, then started a master's in data science. My favorite part of the analyst job was trying to make a ridership dashboard make sense to the agency's board. The SQL behind it was easy. The hard part was guessing what someone would conclude from a chart they'd look at for ten seconds, and whether that conclusion would be right. I didn't know then that this was a research question. I thought it was just part of the job.

When my research project ended, my advisor offered me a follow-up forecasting project. I realized I didn't want it. I liked the people in the lab and I liked the work, but what I actually cared about was everything around the model: whose data went in, who would read the output, and what they would assume.

So I took a Human-Centered Data Science course, and for the first time I studied people directly. My team ran think-aloud sessions with five classmates using an AutoML tool. It was a small class project, but what we saw surprised me. People accepted whichever model the tool ranked first. Nobody opened the panel explaining what the model relied on. Two people assumed the tool already knew which data was sensitive. These were data science students, the people the tool was designed for. They trusted it in exactly the places they shouldn't have. Writing up those findings was the most engaged I've felt in my master's program.

Teaching pointed the same way. For two semesters I was a TA for a statistics course for non-majors. The part of grading I cared about most was the interpretation: whether a student could say what their numbers meant for an actual decision. That's the gap I keep running into, between what data says and what people take from it.

I don't have a finished research agenda, and I'd rather say so than invent one. The questions I keep coming back to are:

- What do people think they agreed to when their data gets reused for something they never imagined?
- Why do people, experts included, defer to an algorithm's top answer?
- What would it take for an interface to help them push back at the right moments?

These questions are about people and AI, not just about building models.

My background is mostly technical, and I know that is both an asset and a gap. I can build the kinds of systems I'd want to study, and I'm comfortable with the statistics. What I don't have yet is serious training in qualitative methods and study design. That's a big part of why I want a PhD rather than another job.
