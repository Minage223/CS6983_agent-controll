# Statement of Purpose: PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences

In 2025 I was a research assistant on a bus ridership forecasting project. At one point I tried a feature that combined two columns. One was the stop where a frequent rider usually first boarded. The other was the area where their phone tended to be at night. Both came from data described as anonymized. Together, they put a lot of riders on just a few blocks.

The feature didn't improve the model, so I dropped it. Nobody did anything wrong. But afterward I looked up how the phone data had been collected. It came from partner apps, through a one-time "allow." I doubt anyone tapping that pictured a transit model, or two columns that together could narrow them down to a block.

That is where my interest in HCI started. Since then I keep coming back to one question: what do people think can be figured out from what they share, and could they ever actually see it? That used to mean apps. Now it also means AI tools that people talk to, about a lot more than where they sleep.

My background is mostly in numbers. I studied economics, then worked for a year as a data analyst at a regional transit agency. I am now finishing a master's in data science. I'm comfortable building models. That feature was when I noticed that the part I cared about wasn't the model.

The closest I have come to studying this directly was a class project in Human-Centered Data Science. My team of three ran think-aloud sessions with five classmates using an AutoML tool. Several things came up, but one stuck. Two participants assumed the tool already knew which columns were sensitive. It didn't.

It felt like the same problem from the other side. People who share data assume not much can be inferred from it. People who build with data assume the system will catch what's sensitive. Neither can see what the system can actually work out, so they fill the gap with an assumption.

It was five people in a class project, and I don't want to make more of it than it is. But it was the first time I had a method for asking the question instead of just having it.

I don't have a clear research question yet. I have a cluster of them, and I expect the first years of a PhD to narrow them down:

- What do people believe can be inferred from the data they share, and how far off are they?
- Can an interface show someone what could be learned from their data, in a way they understand, before they agree to share it?
- How does this change with conversational AI, where people share more, more casually, and the inferences are harder to see?
- On the builder side, what do practitioners assume their tools handle for them, and what happens when they're wrong?

What I bring is time on the side that builds these things. I know how easy it is to combine two harmless-looking columns, and how little anyone downstream would notice. What I lack is training in qualitative methods, study design, and the privacy and HCI literature. That is the main reason I want a PhD rather than another job.
