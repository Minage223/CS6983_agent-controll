Morgan Reyes — Statement of Purpose, PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences

When my research advisor offered me a follow-up project extending our bus ridership model to light rail, I said I'd think about it. Thinking about it made something clear: I didn't want another forecasting project. What had stayed with me from that work wasn't the model. It was one small thing I'd done along the way.

We were using a mobility dataset described as anonymized: phone location data from a vendor, grouped by neighborhood. As an experiment, I combined two columns: where a frequent rider first boarded each day, and where their phone tended to spend the night. Each column was supposedly anonymous. Together, they put many people on a few blocks. The feature didn't improve the model, so I dropped it. Nobody did anything wrong, and the feature was gone. But it kept bugging me. I went back to see how the data had been collected. It came from apps whose users had opted in once, with one tap.

Since then I keep wondering what people think can be figured out from what they share, and whether they could ever actually see it. That question used to be about apps. Now people also talk to AI tools, often in more detail than they'd put into any form, and the distance between what they think they've revealed and what can be inferred from it seems to keep growing.

I saw the same problem from the other side in a Human-Centered Data Science course. My team ran think-aloud sessions with five classmates using an AutoML tool. What got me was how much they trusted it. They accepted whatever model the tool ranked first, and nobody opened the panel that explained its features. Two of them assumed the tool already knew which columns were sensitive. In the mobility data, people trusted that what they shared would stay harmless. In the AutoML sessions, people trusted that the system had already worked out what was sensitive. In both cases, what people believed about a data system didn't match what it was doing, and nothing in front of them showed the difference.

That mismatch is what I want to study in a PhD. Specifically:

- What do people believe can be inferred from what they share, with apps and with AI tools?
- Where are those beliefs wrong?
- Can we design ways for people to see those inferences before they share, not after?

I've spent several years on the other side of this problem, working with data that people agreed to share without really knowing what could be done with it. I know how easily two harmless-looking pieces become something more, and that's the experience I'd bring.

This is why I want to work with Prof. Tianshi Li in the PEACH Lab. Her CHI 2024 study with LLM chatbot users found that wrong mental models and dark patterns kept people from understanding the privacy risks of what they disclosed. That is the gap I keep coming back to. Rescriber, her lab's tool that helps users spot and remove personal information from their prompts, is the kind of intervention I'd like to build and study. Her recent position paper argues that inference and data aggregation get a small share of privacy research attention. My mobility-data experience was a small, concrete example of that problem.
