**Statement of Purpose — Riley Nakamura**
**PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences — Fall 2027**

In the summer of 2025, I was coding about 1,200 open-ended survey responses about AI Coach, a feature at the fitness app Cadence that changes users' workout plans based on their activity, sleep, and heart-rate data. The product manager wanted one sentiment number for the launch deck, and the final figure was 72% positive. One group of responses didn't fit any of my sentiment codes. Those users weren't happy or unhappy with the feature. They *didn't understand why their plan had changed.*

That group ended up in the appendix. I've kept thinking about it, because it pointed to something the rest of my analysis had missed. I had owned the analysis of AI Coach's launch experiment: four weeks, about 180,000 users. Treatment users completed 8.0% more weekly workouts (95% CI 6.1–9.9%), and 7-day retention rose 2.1 percentage points. I checked for a novelty effect, and the lift held through week four. By every metric we tracked, the feature worked. Yet some of the people using it couldn't explain what it was doing to them, and nothing we measured would have caught that.

I want to pursue a PhD to study that gap. My research question is how people understand, and decide whether to trust, AI systems that act on their personal data. I'm also interested in how we should measure that understanding, since engagement metrics can stay high while it's missing.

**Research interests**

I'm drawn to three related questions:

1. **Making sense of adaptive systems.** AI Coach didn't just recommend. It changed users' plans without being asked. When a system adapts on its own, how do people notice the changes, explain them to themselves, and decide whether to go along? How do those explanations change over weeks of use?

2. **Sensitive personal data.** Sleep and heart-rate data feel more personal than clicks. Does the type of data a system uses change how much explanation people expect, or how they react when an inference seems wrong?

3. **Measuring trust and understanding.** In industry, AI features are judged mostly on behavior: retention, engagement, conversion. I want to develop measures that capture whether people understand and appropriately trust these systems, and to test how those measures relate to the behavioral metrics teams already use.

I expect to use mixed methods: interviews and diary studies to see how people's understanding forms over time, plus surveys and field experiments to test what we learn at scale.

**Preparation**

I should be direct about where I'm starting from. I don't have research publications, and my formal research experience comes from my undergraduate HCI coursework at Riverside State University. In User Research Methods, I ran semi-structured interviews with six students about how they used campus apps and synthesized the findings into an affinity diagram and report. For my capstone, my four-person team redesigned the campus library app and ran a usability test with eight students.

My stronger preparation comes from four years as a product data analyst at Cadence. I've owned the analysis for about 20 A/B tests a year: writing analysis plans, running power calculations, and presenting results to product teams. That work taught me to care about study design. Once a PM wanted to run a one-week test on a paywall change. My power calculation showed it needed about four weeks to detect the effect we cared about, so I proposed a three-week test with a pre-registered primary metric, and the PM agreed. I also write SQL and Python (pandas, statsmodels) every day for cohort analysis and modeling.

In 2025 I volunteered to take notes for four interviews our UX research team ran on AI Coach. The researcher led the interviews and the synthesis. My job was capturing what participants said, and I set up a notes format that kept timestamped quotes separate from my interpretations. That month confirmed for me that I wanted to do this kind of work, and I want the training to lead it myself.
