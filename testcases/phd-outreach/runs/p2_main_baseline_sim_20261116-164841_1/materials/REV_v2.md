**Statement of Purpose — Riley Nakamura**
**PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences — Fall 2027**

In 2025 I sat in on four user interviews about AI Coach, a feature in the fitness app where I work as a data analyst. AI Coach changes people's workout plans based on their activity, sleep, and heart-rate data. I was there to take notes. Mostly I was listening to people try to explain, in their own words, what the app was doing for them. It was more interesting than any dashboard I've built.

Around the same time, I was coding about 1,200 open-ended responses from our post-launch survey. Roughly one in ten said something like "it changed my plan and I have no idea why," or asked how the app knew they'd slept badly. A couple of people said they'd turned off health-data sharing. In the launch deck, all of it became one number: 72% positive.

The metric and what people were saying felt like two different products. That still bugs me. It's the main reason I'm applying.

**What I want to study**

I want to understand what people think is happening when an app uses their own data to make choices for them, and what would make them feel they still have a say.

Some questions I keep coming back to:

- When a system changes something without asking, how do people notice? How do they explain it to themselves?
- When does an inference feel helpful, and when does it feel intrusive? Easing someone's workout after a bad night is reasonable. It's also a little unsettling that the app knows.
- When people turn off data sharing, what are they reacting to? The data, the decision, or not being asked?
- What does having a say actually look like? Explanations, controls, the ability to undo, or something else?

I'm not interested in making these systems more engaging or their recommendations more accurate. Plenty of people work on that. I'm interested in the people on the other end.

I expect this work to start with interviews and diary studies, following people over weeks as their understanding forms. Then I'd test what we learn at a larger scale. My questions aren't fully formed yet. Learning how to sharpen them is part of why I want a PhD.

**Where I'm starting from**

I don't have publications. My research experience comes from undergraduate coursework in Riverside State's HCI track: an interview study with six students about campus apps, and a capstone usability test with eight. They were small projects.

Since 2022 I've been a product analyst at Cadence. The job taught me two things. A well-designed experiment can tell you a lot. A clean result can also leave out most of what users actually experienced. I want to do research that takes both seriously.

**Why a PhD**

A research job would mostly have me answering whatever the product team needs that quarter. The questions above don't fit in a quarter, and they aren't always the ones a product team wants answered. I want the time and training to study them properly.
