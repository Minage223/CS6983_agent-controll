**Statement of Interest: PhD in Human-Computer Interaction, Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

My machine learning course project asked a classifier to read the emotional tone of posts in online mental-health support communities. In the error analysis, I found the model's most consistent mistakes came from sarcasm and dark humor. It labeled posts as negative when the writers were often coping, joking with peers, or signaling that they belonged. On paper the model did well, with a macro-F1 of 0.81 against 0.72 for a TF-IDF baseline. But it misread exactly the kind of talk that seems to make these communities feel safe to the people in them. That gap between what a system measures and what people mean is the question I want to pursue in a PhD.

I came to this question through three experiences, and in each I worked in a supporting role. I've learned a lot by watching how careful research and analysis are actually done, and I now want to learn to lead that work myself.

**Qualitative research with teens.** Since October 2025 I have been an undergraduate research assistant in Brookfield University's Social Computing Lab, working with Prof. A. Moreno and PhD student J. Kim. I support J. Kim's interview study of how teens aged 15 to 17 use online communities for mental-health support. I sent recruitment messages to community moderators and ran the eligibility screener. I transcribed and de-identified 14 interviews, replacing names, schools and server names with placeholders. I then coded 6 transcripts with the team's codebook. When J. Kim and I first coded the same transcripts, we disagreed in ways that showed how much interpretation goes into deciding what a teen "means." After one round of reconciliation we reached a Cohen's kappa of 0.71. Those discussions taught me that a code is a claim about a person, and that the claim should be made with care. The team's paper is now under review, and I am listed in the acknowledgments.

**Measurement in industry.** In summer 2026 I interned as a data analyst at Brightpath Learning, an ed-tech company. I built a Tableau dashboard of weekly student engagement and wrote the SQL behind it. I found that sessions were being double-counted whenever students put the app in the background, and I redefined a session as activity with gaps of less than 30 minutes. It was a small fix, but it showed me that every engagement metric starts from a decision about what counts as a person "being there." Product teams act on those decisions, usually without seeing them.

**Computational analysis.** The classifier project, done with two teammates, gave me hands-on experience with NLP. More importantly, it showed me that error analysis is where I learn the most, because it shows where a system's view of people breaks down.

Together, these experiences point me toward a tentative research direction. I'm interested in how computational systems interpret the ways young people talk about distress and support online, and in how designers, moderators and communities can account for what those systems miss. Moderation tools, risk classifiers and engagement metrics all encode assumptions about what distress or participation looks like. I want to study where those assumptions fail, using qualitative methods to understand what people mean and computational methods to find where systems diverge from it. I'm especially curious whether that understanding can lead to tools that support peer communities rather than flatten them. I hold this direction loosely. Part of why I want to do a PhD is to learn how to turn an observation like the one from my classifier project into a well-formed research question.
