Since October 2025 I've been an undergraduate research assistant in the Social Computing Lab at Brookfield University, working with Prof. A. Moreno and PhD student J. Kim. The lab studies how teens use online communities for mental-health support. I've worked on J. Kim's interview study with 16 teens aged 15 to 17. I sent recruitment messages to community moderators and ran the eligibility screener. Over one winter I transcribed and de-identified 14 interviews, replacing names, schools, and community names with placeholders. These were supporting roles, and I learned a lot from doing them carefully. Transcribing showed me how much meaning sits in hesitations, jokes, and things teens almost say. De-identifying showed me how easily a small detail, like the name of a server, could identify a participant, and how much responsibility comes with handling data about minors. Reconciling codes with my mentor taught me that disagreements usually point to something real: a category that doesn't fit, or a statement that can be read in more than one way.

I saw the same problem from a computational angle in a Machine Learning course project. My team of three fine-tuned a transformer to classify sentiment in Reddit mental-health support posts. It reached a macro-F1 of 0.81, compared with 0.72 for a TF-IDF baseline. I led the error analysis, which became the part of the project I cared about most. The model often labeled sarcastic or darkly humorous posts as negative. In a support community, dark humor can be a way of coping or connecting, so the model's mistake wasn't just a technical one. It was a misreading of how people actually talk. A keyword filter that removes a teen's post makes the same kind of mistake, but the person affected sees the result directly.

My internship at Brightpath Learning, an ed-tech company, added a third view. I built a Tableau dashboard of weekly student engagement for the product team and wrote the SQL behind it. Partway through, I found that sessions were being double-counted when students put the app in the background, and I redefined how sessions were measured. A metric like "engagement" depends on choices about what counts, and those choices shape what a team believes about its users.

The question I'm most drawn to right now is this: **when automated systems interpret what vulnerable people say online, what do they miss, and how could those systems be designed around the needs of the people they act on?** This applies to moderation bots, classifiers, and, more and more, AI agents. How can communities protect young people without silencing the ways they support each other?
