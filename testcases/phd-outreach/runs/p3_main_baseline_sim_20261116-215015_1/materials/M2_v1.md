**Statement of Interest: PhD in Human-Computer Interaction, Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

My time in research so far has been spent close to the data: recruiting participants, transcribing interviews, removing identifying details, and coding transcripts line by line. That work hasn't given me a finished research agenda. It has given me questions I keep coming back to about how people, and especially young people, look for support online, and about what happens when researchers and systems try to interpret what they say. I'm applying to the HCI PhD at Khoury to learn how to turn those questions into research of my own.

Since October 2025 I've been an undergraduate research assistant in the Social Computing Lab at Brookfield University, working with Prof. A. Moreno and PhD student J. Kim. The lab studies how teens use online communities for mental-health support. I've worked on J. Kim's interview study with 16 teens aged 15 to 17. I sent recruitment messages to community moderators and ran the eligibility screener. Over one winter I transcribed and de-identified 14 interviews, replacing names, schools, and community names with placeholders. I then coded 6 transcripts with the team's codebook and took part in inter-rater reliability checks, reaching a Cohen's kappa of 0.71 with my mentor after one round of reconciliation. I'm listed in the acknowledgments of the team's paper, which is now under review.

These were supporting roles, and I learned a lot from doing them carefully. Transcribing showed me how much meaning sits in hesitations, jokes, and things teens almost say. De-identifying showed me how easily a small detail, like the name of a server, could identify a participant, and how much responsibility comes with handling data about minors. Reconciling codes with my mentor taught me that disagreements usually point to something real: a category that doesn't fit, or a statement that can be read in more than one way.

I saw the same problem from a computational angle in a Machine Learning course project. My team of three fine-tuned a transformer to classify sentiment in Reddit mental-health support posts. It reached a macro-F1 of 0.81, compared with 0.72 for a TF-IDF baseline. I led the error analysis, which became the part of the project I cared about most. The model often labeled sarcastic or darkly humorous posts as negative. In a support community, dark humor can be a way of coping or connecting, so the model's mistake wasn't just a technical one. It was a misreading of how people actually talk. Having spent hours with teens' own words in interview transcripts, I found that gap hard to ignore.

My internship at Brightpath Learning, an ed-tech company, added a third view. I built a Tableau dashboard of weekly student engagement for the product team and wrote the SQL behind it. Partway through, I found that sessions were being double-counted when students put the app in the background, and I redefined how sessions were measured. A metric like "engagement" depends on choices about what counts, and those choices shape what a team believes about its users.

Together, these experiences point me toward a set of questions:

- How do people in online support communities, especially teens, express distress, humor, and care, and what do automated systems such as moderation tools, classifiers, and engagement metrics miss when they interpret that expression?
- How can we design community spaces and moderation practices that protect young people without flattening the ways they actually support each other?
- How should researchers and platforms handle the privacy of sensitive data from minors, not only in what they collect but in how they analyze it?
