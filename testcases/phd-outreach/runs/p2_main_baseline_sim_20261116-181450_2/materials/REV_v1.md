**Statement of Purpose — PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

A few years into working as a product analyst, I read a survey response I still think about: *I didn't understand why my plan changed.*

The plan belonged to a user of AI Coach, a feature in the fitness app where I work. It rewrites people's workout plans based on their activity, sleep, and heart-rate data. I had analyzed the launch experiment, and by every measure we tracked, the feature worked. People worked out more and stayed longer. Then I helped code the open-ended responses from the post-launch survey, and that sentence kept coming up in different forms. Some of the people writing it were using the app more than before. They just couldn't say what the system had done with their data, or why.

We had no good place for that finding. It didn't fit a sentiment score, and it didn't affect the launch decision, so it went into an appendix. I understood why. What stayed with me was that my usual tools couldn't see it at all. An experiment tells you whether people's behavior changed. It doesn't tell you whether they understand the system that changed it, or whether the trust they're placing in it is deserved. Someone who has thought it through and someone who is just going along can look identical in the data.

That's what I want to study. When an AI system acts on someone's personal data, such as adjusting a plan, flagging a purchase, or moving money, how do people make sense of what it did? Where does their explanation differ from what the system actually did, and does that difference matter? How could we measure understanding and trust carefully enough that the answer affects decisions instead of ending up in an appendix?

I don't think more analytics will answer those questions. I learned that in 2025, when I volunteered to take notes for four interviews our UX researcher ran with AI Coach users. I had only ever seen these people as rows in a table. In the interviews, they explained the feature in their own words and described theories about how it worked that I'd never have guessed from the logs. It was the most interesting part of my work that year. It also made clear that the question I cared about was one I couldn't answer from my current job.

My research experience is limited, and I won't pretend otherwise. I have no publications. In my undergraduate HCI program, I ran a small interview study and a usability test for a class project. Since then I've spent four years working closely with experiments: designing them, checking whether they can answer the question being asked, and explaining what they do and don't show. I want to keep that rigor and add what I'm missing. That means learning to design qualitative studies, to build theory instead of only testing it, and to measure things that are harder to define than a click.

**Why Khoury**

I hope to work with Professor Tianshi Li. Her lab studies what happens when AI systems use people's personal data and how design can give people more understanding and control. Her COLM 2026 paper with Zhang, Zhang, and Shi found that personalization raised privacy concerns and lowered trust in LLM agents less when the agent handed control back to the user at risky moments. In other words, people's sense of control shaped how they felt about a system acting for them. "Why am I seeing this," a UIST 2025 paper she co-authored, starts from the observation that people form theories about why a personalized system showed them something but have no way to check them. That's very close to what AI Coach users were telling us. I'd like to bring these questions to features that act on people's health and money, where getting it wrong matters most.

I'm applying now because I've spent four years measuring what AI features do *to* people, and I want to understand what those features mean *to* them.
