**Statement of Purpose — PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

In July 2025 I was coding open-ended survey responses about AI Coach, a feature I'd spent the previous month evaluating. AI Coach is a fitness app feature that rewrites a user's workout plan based on their activity, sleep, and heart-rate data. By the measures my team cared about, it worked. In a four-week launch experiment with about 180,000 users, it increased weekly workouts by 8% (95% CI 6.1–9.9%) and 7-day retention by 2.1 percentage points, and the effect held through the fourth week. I owned that analysis. When the post-launch survey came back, the product manager asked for one sentiment number for the leadership deck: 72% positive.

While I was coding the roughly 1,200 responses, one theme kept appearing that didn't fit "positive," "neutral," or "negative": *I didn't understand why my plan changed.* Some of these people were working out more. Some were satisfied. They still couldn't tell what the system had done with their data or why. I asked whether the theme should be reported separately, and it went into the appendix. That decision made sense for a launch readout. But I came away with a question the readout couldn't answer, and it's the question I want to spend a PhD on:

**When an AI system acts on someone's personal data, how do they make sense of what it did? And how can we tell whether they understand and trust it, when engagement metrics can look excellent either way?**

**Why this question, and why it needs research**

Adaptive AI features are now common in consumer apps that handle intimate data: fitness, health, finances, sleep. Companies usually judge them with the tools I use every day, which are A/B tests on behavioral metrics. I've analyzed about 20 of these experiments a year for four years. I trust the methods, and I've seen their limits. An experiment can show that a feature changes behavior. It can't show whether people understand the system acting on them, whether that understanding matches what the system actually does, or whether their trust is well placed. An 8% lift is consistent with users who understand the feature and with users who are just going along with it. Those outcomes look the same on a dashboard and mean very different things.

I'm interested in three related directions:

1. **How people make sense of adaptive changes.** When a system changes something on a user's behalf, such as a workout plan, a budget, or a savings transfer, what explanations do people come up with? When do those explanations match how the system works?
2. **Measuring understanding and trust.** Can we build measures of user understanding and calibrated trust that are rigorous enough to sit next to engagement metrics in product decisions, so they don't end up in an appendix?
3. **Combining behavioral logs and qualitative data.** How can large-scale interaction data help researchers decide whom to interview and when, for example right after an unexpected change? And how can qualitative findings help explain what the logs show?

These ideas are still early, and I expect them to change through coursework and working with an advisor. I'm confident about the core gap, though, because I've seen it from inside a product team.

**What I bring**

My research training is modest, and I want to be clear about that. I don't have publications. My formal research experience comes from my B.S. in Information Science (HCI track) at Riverside State University. In my User Research Methods course, I ran semi-structured interviews with six students about how they used campus apps and synthesized them with an affinity diagram. In my capstone, my team of four redesigned the campus library app and ran a usability test with eight students.

Since graduating, I've built strong quantitative and experimental skills in industry. As a Product Data Analyst at Cadence, I write analysis plans and power calculations before tests launch. I've caught a sample ratio mismatch caused by a client-side redirect and recommended rerunning the test rather than reweighting the results. I've pushed back on an underpowered one-week test and got agreement on a three-week test with a pre-registered primary metric. For ratio metrics, I've built user-level bootstrap confidence intervals and checked them against the delta method. I work in SQL and Python (pandas, statsmodels) every day.

In 2025 I volunteered to take notes for four user interviews our UX research team ran on AI Coach. I set up the notes to make synthesis easier, with timestamped quotes and observations kept separate from interpretation. Watching people explain in their own words a system I knew mostly through aggregate metrics was the most interesting part of my work that year. It's also when I realized I wanted to ask these questions as a researcher rather than as the analyst reporting a lift.
