**Statement of Purpose**
Riley Nakamura · PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences, Northeastern University · Fall 2027

In July 2025, I was coding about 1,200 open-ended survey responses about AI Coach, a feature at Cadence, the consumer fitness app where I work as a product data analyst. AI Coach changes users' workout plans based on their activity, sleep, and heart-rate data. By the measures we had planned, it worked. I analyzed its four-week launch test with about 180,000 users and found an 8% increase in weekly workouts and a 2.1-point lift in 7-day retention. But one group of responses didn't fit our coding scheme. These users weren't positive or negative about the feature; they were confused. They didn't understand why their plan had changed. The experiment showed that people worked out more. It couldn't show whether they understood what the system was doing for them, or agreed with it. That gap is what I want to study in a PhD.

**Research interests**

AI features that quietly adapt based on personal data are now common. They show up in fitness, health, and personal finance products. I want to understand how people make sense of these systems when they change something, and how that understanding affects trust, reliance, and long-term use. Three questions interest me most:

1. **How do people explain adaptive changes to themselves?** When a system changes a plan, a budget, or a recommendation without saying why, people form their own theories about what happened. I want to know what those theories look like, when they're accurate, and when a wrong theory leads people to over-trust or abandon the system.
2. **How can we measure understanding alongside behavior?** My work has shown me that engagement can rise while understanding stays low, and standard experiment metrics won't catch it. I'm interested in measures of user understanding and well-calibrated trust that can sit next to behavioral outcomes in field experiments.
3. **How do the stakes change things?** A confusing change to a workout plan is an annoyance. A confusing change to someone's savings or medication reminders is not. I want to study whether people need different explanations, and different amounts of control, as the stakes rise.

These questions are still loose, and I expect them to sharpen through coursework and work with faculty.

**What I bring**

For four years at Cadence, I've owned the analysis for about 20 A/B tests a year: analysis plans, power calculations, and readouts. That work has made me careful about measurement, because I've seen how measurement choices shape what a team believes. When I found a 52/48 traffic split in an onboarding test, I confirmed the sample ratio mismatch, traced it to a redirect bug that dropped some Android users, and argued for rerunning the test instead of reweighting the data. When a PM proposed a one-week test that couldn't detect the effect we cared about, I negotiated a longer test with a pre-registered primary metric. For ratio metrics, I implemented user-level bootstrap confidence intervals and checked them against the delta method. On AI Coach, I checked week by week for a novelty effect before we trusted the lift. These experiences taught me to ask what a number can and can't support, and I want to bring that habit to research on human-AI interaction.

My qualitative training is less developed, and it's the part I most want to build. As an undergraduate in Information Science (HCI track), I ran a semi-structured interview study with six students, synthesized it with affinity diagramming, and ran a usability test with eight participants for my capstone. At Cadence, I volunteered to take notes for four interviews that our UX research team ran on AI Coach. I worked out a note format that kept timestamped quotes and kept what we observed separate from how we interpreted it. Watching the researcher's follow-up questions uncover reasons that no survey item had captured was the most interesting part of my job that year. That experience convinced me that the questions I care about need both methods: experiments to show what people do, and interviews and diary studies to show why.

I haven't published or led an independent research study yet. A PhD is where I want to learn to do that well.
