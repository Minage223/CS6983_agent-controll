**Statement of Purpose**
Riley Nakamura · PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences, Northeastern University · Fall 2027

In the summer of 2025, I was helping code about 1,200 open-ended survey responses about AI Coach, a feature at Cadence, the fitness app where I work as a data analyst. AI Coach changes your workout plan based on your activity, sleep, and heart-rate data. By the numbers, the launch went well: people in the test worked out about 8% more each week. But in the written responses, maybe one in ten people said something that didn't fit that picture. Their plan had changed and they had no idea why. Some asked how the app knew they'd slept badly. A couple had turned off health-data sharing.

In the final deck, all of it was folded into a single "72% positive." I understood why. The team needed a number to make a launch decision. But the +8% and what people actually wrote felt like two different products. The first showed that the feature changed what people did. The second showed that many of them couldn't tell what it was doing, or why it seemed to know so much about them.

A few months later, I took notes for interviews our UX research team ran about AI Coach. There wasn't one dramatic quote. What stayed with me was hearing people explain, in their own words, what they thought the app was doing with their data. Their explanations were specific, sometimes wrong, and clearly shaped how they felt about using it. Hearing those explanations was the best part of my month.

That's the question I want to study: **what do people think is going on when an app uses their own data to decide things for them, and what would make them feel they still have a say?**

More and more everyday products work this way. Fitness apps adjust your training, finance apps move your money, and health apps decide what to remind you about. Usually they don't explain themselves, so people form their own understanding of what the system knows and why it acts. I want to study what that understanding looks like and where it comes from. I also want to find out what kinds of explanation, control, or ability to push back make people feel like participants rather than subjects. Privacy is part of this. Sometimes the question really is "how did it know that?" But it's not the whole picture. People who are fine with sharing their data may still want to understand and shape what's done with it.

My path to this question has run through measurement. For four years, I've analyzed experiments and built the metrics my team uses to judge features. That work taught me to be careful about what a number can actually support. It also showed me what numbers leave out. I don't want to study how to make people engage more. I want to understand the people behind the engagement numbers. My research experience so far is modest: an interview study with affinity mapping and a usability test from my undergraduate HCI coursework, plus the interviews I sat in on at Cadence. I haven't published or led my own study. I'm applying because I want to learn to do this research well. That means combining interviews, diary studies, and surveys with the experimental work I already know, so that what people say about these systems is taken as seriously as what they do.

I'm especially interested in working with Professor Tianshi Li. Her research on how AI autonomy and personalization affect people's sense of control, and on the mental models people form about AI systems that handle their personal data, is close to the questions I want to pursue. What I took from AI Coach is that a feature can succeed by every measure we track while many of its users don't know what it's doing to them. I want to spend the next several years understanding that gap, and helping design systems that close it.
