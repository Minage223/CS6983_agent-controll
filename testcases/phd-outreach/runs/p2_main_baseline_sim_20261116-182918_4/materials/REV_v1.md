**Statement of Purpose**
Riley Nakamura · PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences, Northeastern University · Fall 2027

What I remember most about AI Coach isn't the launch result. It's a handful of survey responses that didn't fit anywhere.

AI Coach is a feature at Cadence, the fitness app where I work. It changes your workout plan based on your activity, sleep, and heart-rate data. After launch, I helped sort about 1,200 written survey responses into positive, neutral, and negative. Most were easy to sort. But a group of people were saying something else: they didn't understand why their plan had changed. They weren't happy or unhappy. They were confused, and our categories had no place for that. I asked whether we should report them separately, and they ended up in an appendix. That was a reasonable choice for the product, but I kept thinking about those responses.

A few months later, I volunteered to take notes for interviews our UX research team ran about AI Coach. [**Your words: one specific moment from those interviews that stayed with you, such as something a participant said or did, or a follow-up question that changed how you saw things.**] It was the most interesting part of my month. The survey had told us that people were confused. The interviews started to show what they thought was going on.

That's the question I want to spend a PhD on. More and more products quietly adapt to you based on data you never consciously handed over. When they don't explain themselves, people fill in the gap with their own theories. I want to understand what those theories look like, when they're wrong, and what that does to trust. Do people end up relying on a system they shouldn't, or giving up on one that was actually helping them? I also suspect the answer depends on the stakes. A confusing change to a workout plan is a shrug. A confusing change to your savings or your health is not.

I come to this from the measurement side, and that's part of why it matters to me. For four years, I've helped my team judge products by what people do: whether they come back and whether they work out more. I've learned to be careful about what a number can support. I've argued for rerunning a test instead of patching its data, and for running tests long enough to answer the question being asked. But AI Coach showed me the limit. People's behavior improved, and I still couldn't say whether they understood or trusted what the system was doing. [**Your words: whether that feels true to you, or what you'd say instead.**]

My research experience so far is small: an interview study with affinity mapping and a usability test from my undergraduate HCI coursework, plus sitting in on those interviews at Cadence. I haven't published or led my own study. That's why I'm applying. I want to learn to do this kind of research properly, combining interviews and experiments instead of choosing one.

I'm especially interested in working with Professor Tianshi Li. Her work on how autonomy and personalization shape users' trust and sense of control in AI agents is close to my questions about systems that act on personal data. [**Add one or two more faculty after you check them.**]
