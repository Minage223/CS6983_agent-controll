# Statement of Purpose
**Avery Santos — PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences, Northeastern University**

Last fall, my team of three built a sentiment classifier for a Machine Learning course. We fine-tuned a transformer on Reddit mental-health support posts, and it beat our baseline, with a macro-F1 of 0.81 against 0.72. The part I've kept thinking about came from the error analysis, which I did: the model kept labeling sarcastic and dark-humor posts as negative. Some of those posts read like people joking their way through something hard. A system that reads them literally gets them wrong, and that would matter if the system were deciding what to flag, hide or escalate in a support community. I want to do a PhD in HCI to study that gap between what computational systems measure and what people in online support communities actually mean and need.

**Research experience**

Since October 2025 I have been an undergraduate research assistant in the Social Computing Lab at Brookfield University, working with Prof. A. Moreno and PhD student J. Kim. The lab studies how teens use online communities for mental-health support, along with peer support, moderation and the design of safer community spaces. I've contributed to J. Kim's interview study with 16 teens aged 15–17. I wasn't the lead, but I worked on almost every stage of the data pipeline, and each stage taught me something about doing this kind of research well.

During recruitment I wrote outreach messages to community moderators and ran the eligibility screener. I also watched how carefully the study handled parental consent and teen assent. I then transcribed and de-identified 14 interviews, replacing names, schools and community names with placeholders. That work made it clear to me that protecting participants takes many small decisions, not one step. Finally, I coded 6 transcripts with the team's codebook. In an inter-rater reliability check with J. Kim, we reached a Cohen's kappa of 0.71 after one round of reconciliation. Writing memos about the cases where we disagreed showed me how much interpretation goes into turning what someone says into data. The team's paper is now under review, and I'm listed in the acknowledgments.

My industry experience pointed me to the same issue from the other side. As a data analyst intern at Brightpath Learning, an ed-tech company, I built a Tableau dashboard of weekly student engagement and wrote the SQL behind it. I found that sessions were being counted twice whenever students sent the app to the background, so I redefined a session as activity with gaps under 30 minutes. It was a small fix, but it changed what the numbers said about how students used the product. I came away more aware that how we define a measure shapes the conclusions people draw from it.

**Research interests**

These experiences come together in a set of questions I'd like to pursue:

- How do automated tools, such as sentiment classifiers and moderation systems, interpret the ways young people express distress online, including humor, irony and community-specific language? Where do they get it wrong?
- How do peer moderators and community members understand and work around these tools?
- How might support communities be designed so that automated systems support human judgment instead of overriding it?

I'd like to approach these questions with mixed methods, pairing interviews and qualitative analysis with computational analysis of community data. My background suits that pairing: I've trained models and done error analysis, and I've also transcribed and coded interviews. I'm still early in shaping these questions, and I'm looking for a program where I can sharpen them with advisors who work at this intersection.
