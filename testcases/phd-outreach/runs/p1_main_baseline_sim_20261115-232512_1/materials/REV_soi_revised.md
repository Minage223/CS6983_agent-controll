# Statement of Purpose: Morgan Reyes
**PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences, Fall 2027**

The mobility data our lab used came from apps on people's phones. According to the vendor's documentation, users had agreed to share it through a one-time opt-in. My first reaction when I read that was: *I doubt anyone tapping "allow" pictured this.*

I was a research assistant at the time, testing features for a bus ridership model. One feature combined where frequent riders first boarded with nighttime mobility patterns. It didn't help the model, so I dropped it. Before I did, I noticed that it concentrated many riders into just a few blocks. Nothing went wrong. The data was anonymized and aggregated, and the feature never went anywhere. But I've thought about that moment more than about anything in the paper we published.

What stayed with me wasn't really a question about data. It was a question about people: what did they think they were agreeing to? Somewhere between an "allow" button and a model's feature set, there's a gap between what a person imagines and what actually happens. I'd spent a long time on the technical side of that gap without ever asking about the other side.

## Seeing it again

In a Human-Centered Data Science course last spring, my team ran think-aloud sessions with five classmates using an AutoML tool. Nearly everything we saw was some form of misplaced trust. Everyone accepted the tool's top-ranked model without looking further. No one opened the explanations. Two participants assumed the tool already knew which columns in their data were sensitive.

That last finding felt familiar. It was the consent prompt again, this time from the user's side: someone trusting a system to be doing something it wasn't. It was a small class project, and I know one usability study is a starting point, not a background. But it was the first time I'd studied this gap directly instead of just noticing it, and I wanted to keep going.

## Where I'm coming from

I came to HCI through data science, and I won't pretend otherwise. I worked as an analyst at a transit agency, built ridership forecasting models as a research assistant, and spent this past summer building a churn model at an insurance company. That work taught me how data actually gets collected, joined, aggregated, and turned into something a model can use. That's why I find the gap between those steps and what people picture so specific and so hard to ignore.

When I try to describe what I want to work on next, I keep landing on the same phrase: something with people and AI, not just building models.

## What I want to study

More concretely, I want to understand what people believe data systems are doing, both with their data and on their behalf, and what happens when those beliefs are wrong. How does someone who tapped "allow" once picture their data being used a year later? Could disclosures or consent designs change that picture in a meaningful way, not just on paper? When people use ML tools, which protections do they assume are built in, and how should tools be honest about what they don't do?

I don't have a full research agenda yet, and I'd rather say that than invent one. What I do have is a set of questions I keep coming back to, technical experience that helps me make those questions concrete, and a real need for the methods I haven't learned yet: interviewing, qualitative analysis, and study design. Those are what I'm hoping to learn at Khoury.
