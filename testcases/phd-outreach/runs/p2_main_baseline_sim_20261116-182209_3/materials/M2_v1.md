# Statement of Purpose: PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences

In the summer of 2025 I was coding about 1,200 open-ended survey responses about AI Coach, a feature my company had just launched. AI Coach changes users' workout plans automatically based on their activity, sleep, and heart-rate data. The product team wanted one sentiment number for the launch deck, and the answer was 72% positive. As I worked through the responses, though, I kept seeing a theme the sentiment scheme couldn't hold: people who "didn't understand why the plan changed." Some were curious, some were annoyed, and some had stopped trusting the plan. None of them belonged cleanly in positive, neutral, or negative. I suggested reporting the theme separately. It ended up in the appendix.

That theme has stayed with me more than anything else in my four years as a product data analyst, and it's the reason I'm applying to the HCI PhD program at Khoury. I want to study how people make sense of AI systems that act on their personal data, and how we can measure that understanding instead of assuming engagement metrics capture it.

## The question

I also analyzed the AI Coach launch experiment. In a four-week test with about 180,000 users, the treatment group did 8% more weekly workouts (95% CI 6.1–9.9%) and had 2.1 points higher 7-day retention. I checked the effect week by week for novelty, and it held through week four. By every standard my team used, the feature worked, and it was rolled out to all users.

Both findings were true at once. The behavioral metrics showed people using the feature more, while a meaningful group of those same people couldn't explain what it was doing. Industry has good tools for the first kind of evidence and weak tools for the second. Engagement and retention can't tell apart a user who follows an adaptive plan because they understand and agree with it from one who follows it because they've stopped asking questions. In fitness, that difference affects whether someone trusts the system enough to push back when a recommendation is wrong for their body. In domains like personal finance, where AI assistants now move money automatically, the stakes are higher.

Questions I want to pursue include:

- **How do people form and revise mental models of systems that adapt to them?** What happens to that model when the system changes its behavior without explanation, and which kinds of changes, at what moments, lead to confusion, overreliance, or abandonment?
- **How can we measure understanding and appropriate trust at scale?** Can lightweight, in-product instruments, validated against interviews and diary studies, capture something that logs alone can't? How do those measures relate to behavioral outcomes over time?
- **When does explanation help?** Under what conditions does explaining an adaptive change improve people's ability to calibrate their reliance, and when is it noise?

I expect these questions to sharpen during the PhD. What I'm sure about is the shape of the problem: the gap between what people do with AI systems and what they understand about them, studied using both behavioral data and people's own accounts.

## What I bring

I don't have publications. My formal research experience comes from my undergraduate HCI coursework: a semi-structured interview study with six students analyzed through affinity mapping, and a capstone redesign of a campus library app evaluated with an eight-person usability test. I know this is a modest research record, and I've tried to make the most of the research-adjacent work in my job.

Day to day, I own the analysis for about 20 A/B tests a year at Cadence, a consumer fitness app. That includes writing analysis plans, running power calculations, and presenting readouts to product teams. The work has taught me to take threats to validity seriously and to defend those concerns when it's inconvenient. When an onboarding experiment showed a 52/48 split instead of 50/50, I confirmed a sample ratio mismatch, traced it to a redirect bug that was dropping some Android users, and recommended rerunning the test instead of reweighting the results. When a PM wanted a one-week test of a paywall change, my power analysis showed it couldn't detect the effect we cared about, and I negotiated a longer test with a pre-registered primary metric. I work in SQL and Python (pandas, statsmodels) every day.

In 2025, I volunteered to take notes for four interviews our UX research team ran on AI Coach. The researcher led the sessions and the synthesis. My job was to capture timestamped quotes and keep observations separate from interpretations. It was the most interesting work I did that year, because the interviews explained things the experiment data couldn't. It also showed me what I don't yet know how to do: design a qualitative study from scratch, build theory from it, and connect it rigorously to quantitative evidence. I want to learn those skills through doctoral training.

## Why a PhD, and why Khoury

My industry role puts me close to these questions, but it isn't built to answer them. Product research ends at the launch decision. Once AI Coach shipped, the "didn't understand why" question had no owner. Nobody's job was to find out what those users believed the system was doing or what that meant for them. I want to spend the next several years on questions like that one and produce work that applies beyond a single product.
