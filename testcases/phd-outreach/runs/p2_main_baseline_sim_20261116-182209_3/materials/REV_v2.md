# Statement of Purpose: PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences

Most of my job is counting what people do. In the summer of 2025 I spent a few weeks reading what they said instead. It was about 1,200 survey responses about AI Coach, a feature in our fitness app that rewrites people's workout plans using their activity, sleep and heart-rate data. The team needed one sentiment number, and I gave them one. But the responses I kept rereading didn't fit anywhere.

About one in ten said some version of "it changed my plan and I don't know why" or "how does it know I slept badly?" A couple of those people turned off health-data sharing afterward. In the launch deck, all of it was rolled into "72% positive." I asked whether we should report those responses separately, and they went in the appendix.

I wasn't surprised. I had also analyzed the launch experiment, and by every measure we used, the feature worked: people did more workouts and stuck around longer. It shipped to everyone. The metrics and what people said felt like descriptions of two different products, and the job only asked about one of them. I was a little annoyed. Mostly I wanted to know what those people thought was going on.

A few months later I took notes for four interviews our UX research team ran on AI Coach. You could hear people guessing at what the app was doing with their data and whether they could still steer it. No dashboard I've built shows that. It was the most interesting thing I did that month.

## What I want to study

I want to understand what people think is happening when an app uses their own data to make choices for them, and what would make them feel they still have a say.

Privacy is part of that, but not all of it. The people who turned off health-data sharing may have been protecting their privacy. They may also have been trying to steer the only way the app allowed. I don't know which, and I suspect they didn't fully know either. That's the kind of question I want to work on:

- What do people believe an adaptive system knows about them, and how do they figure it out when it doesn't tell them?
- When the system changes something, what makes it feel like a decision made with them rather than for them?
- When people feel they've lost control, what do they do? Do they override it, stop sharing data, or quietly stop trusting it? And do those reactions ever show up in the metrics?

I'm less interested in making these systems more engaging or more accurate than in what it's like to be on the other end of one.

## Where I'm starting from

I don't have publications. My research experience is undergraduate coursework in my HCI track: an interview study with six students and a usability test with eight.

What I do have is four years with behavioral data. I run the analysis for about 20 experiments a year, write SQL every day, and have learned to distrust results that look too clean. That's half of what this work needs. The other half is designing qualitative studies, building theory from them and connecting them carefully to behavioral data. I've seen it done up close, and it's what I want to learn.

I've spent four years measuring what people do with an app. I'd like to spend the next several finding out what they think it's doing with their data, and what it would take for them to feel they still have a say.
