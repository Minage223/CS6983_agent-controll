Statement of Purpose: PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences

A couple of years ago, I was sitting with a spreadsheet of mobility data trying to make a bus forecast slightly more accurate. I'd built a feature that combined where a frequent rider first boarded with where their phone usually spent the night. It didn't help the model, so I deleted it. But first I looked at what it had produced, and a lot of riders were pinned to just a few blocks.

I went and read how the data was collected. It came from apps whose users had tapped "allow" once, at some point, for some other reason. I remember thinking that I doubted anyone tapping "allow" pictured this.

Nobody had done anything wrong. The data was anonymized, the people in it had agreed, and I'd deleted the feature anyway. That's what bothered me. Everything was fine on paper, and it still wasn't what those people would have expected. I didn't study it or write it up. I went back to lag features and finished the project. But I've thought about that moment more than about the paper the project turned into.

**The same gap, from different sides**

Once I started noticing it, I saw the same gap in a lot of places: a difference between what a data system actually does and what the people around it think it does.

At the transit authority where I worked before graduate school, I made dashboards for planners and board members. The numbers were usually right. What surprised me was how differently people read them. A noisy week looked like a trend, and someone always asked which number was "the real one." I spent more time trying to make a chart honest to a non-expert than making it correct.

Later, presenting a model at an internship, I spent most of my preparation not on the model but on one question: how do I explain this without making it sound more certain than it is?

The clearest version came in a Human-Centered Data Science course last spring. Two classmates and I watched five data science students use an AutoML tool and think aloud. They took the top-ranked model without checking it. No one opened the panel that explained what the model relied on. Two of them assumed the tool already knew which columns were sensitive and would deal with them. These were people trained to be skeptical of models, and the interface's polish quietly did their thinking for them. It reminded me of the riders tapping "allow." In both cases, people trusted that something was being handled on their behalf, and it wasn't, or not the way they imagined.

That project was small, and it was my first time doing this kind of research. It was also the first time an assignment felt like the question I actually cared about, rather than a step on the way to a better number.

**What I want to learn to study**

I don't have a fixed topic yet. The questions I keep coming back to are about people and data systems, not about building better models:

- What do people think happens to data they agree to share, and could consent be designed around what really happens to it later?
- When a tool seems to take care of something, like flagging sensitive data or validating a model, how does that change what people check for themselves?
- How do people who aren't data experts decide how much to trust a number they've been handed?

I came to these questions from the building side. My degrees are in economics and data science, and most of my experience is in making models and dashboards. I know that can make my background look like the wrong fit. I see it as the reason I'm applying. I've been the person upstream making small, reasonable decisions that the people downstream never see. Now I want the methods to study those people properly, instead of guessing about them from a spreadsheet. I know I have a lot to learn about qualitative and experimental research, and that's a big part of why I want a PhD rather than another job.
