Statement of Purpose: PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences

When I worked as a data analyst at a regional transit authority, my job was to keep the ridership dashboards that the planning department used every week. The numbers were usually right. What I couldn't control was how people read them. Planners treated a noisy week as a trend, and board members asked me which number was "the real one." I spent as much time redesigning a dashboard so board members could read it as I did writing the SQL behind it. I've since built forecasting models, published one, and presented one to a VP, and I keep running into the same question: once a model produces a number, how do people decide what to do with it? I want to pursue a PhD in HCI to study that question directly. I'm especially interested in how non-expert users judge, trust, and act on the outputs of data and AI tools, and how those tools could be designed to support better judgment.

**Building models, and watching them get used**

As a research assistant in Lakeshore University's Urban Analytics Lab (PI: Prof. S. Patel), I built gradient-boosted models to forecast bus ridership recovery from four years of passenger counter data and anonymized mobility data. I led the feature engineering and evaluation and wrote the first draft of our paper, which I presented at the Workshop on Urban Data Science (UrbanDS 2025). The review process taught me a lot. One reviewer asked for a seasonal baseline. Another suspected leakage from our mobility features, so I re-ran the models with a longer lag and showed the result still held. I learned to treat an evaluation as something you have to defend, not just report.

The paper's motivation, though, was about people. Planners were making service decisions under deep uncertainty about post-pandemic ridership. Our paper reported accuracy. It didn't ask whether a planner looking at our forecast would understand how far to trust it, or would plan differently because of it. That gap is what I want to study.

I saw the same gap from the other side as a data science intern at Northpoint Insurance. My churn model beat the existing baseline (validation AUC 0.71 to 0.76), and the most demanding part of the project was the final presentation to the retention team and a VP. I had to decide how to explain AUC, what to say about calibration, and how to show which customers the model flagged without suggesting more certainty than it had. I made those choices by instinct, and I'd like to be able to make them with evidence.

**A first look at the question as research**

In Spring 2026 I took Human-Centered Data Science. With two teammates, I ran think-aloud usability sessions with five classmates using an AutoML dashboard, and I wrote the findings section of our report. The study was small and unpublished, but the results stayed with me. Participants accepted the top-ranked model without checking its metrics. None of them opened the feature-importance panel. Two assumed the tool "knew" which columns were sensitive and would handle them. These were data science students, not novices. The interface's ranking and polish seemed to stand in for their own judgment.

That project was my first experience with qualitative methods. It showed me that the questions I'd been asking informally at work could be studied rigorously, and that I have a lot to learn about how to do it well.

Teaching pointed me the same way. As a TA for STAT 1100, a statistics course for non-majors, I graded final data projects where the hardest skill for students wasn't computing a result but interpreting it: saying what a number does and doesn't mean. I helped build the grading rubric around that distinction.

**What I want to study**

I'm drawn to questions such as:

- How do domain experts who aren't data scientists, such as transit planners, decide how much to rely on a forecast or model recommendation? What role do presentation choices like uncertainty displays, rankings, and explanations play?
- When AutoML and AI-assisted analysis tools make modeling easy, what happens to users' scrutiny of the results, and what design interventions bring it back without adding friction no one will tolerate?
- What do users assume a tool is doing on their behalf (for example, handling sensitive data), and how do those assumptions shape their decisions?

My topic isn't fixed, and I expect my first years of the PhD to sharpen it. What I bring is unusual for an HCI applicant: I've built the kinds of models these questions are about, deployed them in real organizations, and presented them to the people who had to act on them. I know where the uncertainty in a forecast comes from and what gets lost when it becomes a single number on a dashboard. I hope to combine that technical grounding with the qualitative and experimental methods I'm still learning.
