Statement of Purpose: PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences

A couple of years ago, I was working on a bus ridership forecast and trying out features built from anonymized phone location data. One combined where a frequent rider first boarded with where their phone usually spent the night. It didn't help the model, so I deleted it. Before I did, I looked at what it had produced, and a lot of riders were pinned to just a few blocks.

I went and read how the data was collected. It came from apps whose users had tapped "allow" once, for some other purpose. I remember thinking that I doubted anyone tapping "allow" pictured this. Then I went back to work. Nobody on the team had done anything wrong, and at the time it was only a passing thought.

It kept coming back, though. The thing I keep wondering about is what people think can be figured out from what they share, and whether they would ever actually get to see it.

**From apps to chatbots**

The riders in that dataset shared their location without knowing it could hint at where they slept. Today, people are sharing far more, and more directly. They tell AI chatbots about their health, their relationships, their jobs, and their worries, in their own words. I don't know what people imagine can be inferred from those conversations, or whether that picture is anywhere close to reality. I suspect the gap is large, and that it's mostly invisible to the people on the sharing side. Usually the only people who see what the data reveals are the ones working with it, the way I was.

That's the question I want to spend a PhD on. I liked building models. I still do. But this question is the one that stuck.

**Where I've seen it**

Most of my experience is on the building side. I worked as an analyst at a transit authority, did research on ridership forecasting, and interned as a data scientist. That work taught me how small and reasonable each step of a data pipeline feels from the inside, and how little of it is visible from the outside.

The first time I studied the outside was in a Human-Centered Data Science course last spring. Two classmates and I watched five data science students use an AutoML tool while thinking aloud. Two of them assumed the tool already knew which columns were sensitive and would handle them. It didn't. These were people trained to be skeptical of models, and they still filled the gap between what they could see and what the system was doing with a comfortable assumption. It was a small class project, but it was the first time research felt aimed at the question I actually cared about. It also showed me how much I still have to learn about methods for studying people.

**What I'd like to study**

I don't have a fixed topic, and I don't plan to continue forecasting or mobility modeling. The questions I keep coming back to are:

- What do people believe can be inferred from what they share, with apps or in conversations with AI chatbots, and how does that compare to what actually can be?
- Would people want to see those inferences? If they could, would it change what they share or how they feel about it?
- How could systems show people what their data reveals, in ways that are understandable rather than another "allow" button?

My background is in economics and data science, and I know that can make me look like the wrong fit for HCI. But I've been the person who sees the inferences people never see. Now I want to learn to study that gap from the other side, with the people it's about.
