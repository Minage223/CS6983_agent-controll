**Statement of Interest: PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

---

The question I keep coming back to started with a feature I threw away.

I was a research assistant on a bus ridership project, and one afternoon I tried combining two columns from our anonymized data: the stop where a regular rider usually got on, and where their phone usually was at night. Together, those two columns put a lot of riders on just a few blocks. The feature didn't help the model, so I deleted it, and that was the end of it as far as the project was concerned. Nobody had done anything wrong.

But I couldn't stop thinking about it. The phone data came from partner apps, where people had tapped "allow" once. I doubt anyone tapping "allow" pictured this. I didn't think they were careless. I thought there was no way for them to know. What can be inferred from data depends on what it's combined with, by whom, and years later. None of that is visible when you tap the button, and as far as I could tell, it never becomes visible afterward.

That afternoon gave me two questions I still have. What do people think can be inferred from what they share? And could they ever actually see what is inferred? Since then, the questions have grown, because people now share things with AI tools they talk to that they would never have typed into a form. They tell a chatbot about their health, their job, their relationships, often in passing, to get a better answer. I want to understand what people imagine is happening to that information, where that picture is wrong, and whether a tool could show them, honestly and in a way they could act on.

I came to these questions from the other side of the data. I've spent most of my working life on the analyst's end: first at a regional transit agency, then in a research lab, now in a data science master's program. I know how easy a join is. But I've noticed that what I care about has been moving away from the model and toward the people around it. The clearest moment came in a Human-Centered Data Science course last spring. My team ran think-aloud sessions with five classmates using an AutoML tool. Two of them assumed the tool "knew" which columns were sensitive and had taken care of them. It hadn't. These were data science students, people who should have known better, and they still assumed the system understood their data better than it did. It was a small class project, but it was the first time I had studied how people think about data rather than the data itself. I didn't want to stop.

I'm early in this, and I know it. My training is mostly in statistics and modeling. I've run one small qualitative study, and I want to learn to do interviews, experiments, and design work properly, not just bolt them onto quantitative analysis. That's a large part of why I'm applying for a PhD rather than another job building models.

I'm especially drawn to Prof. Tianshi Li's work at the PEACH Lab. Her study of how people navigate disclosure in LLM chatbots ("It's a Fair Game," CHI 2024) asks very nearly the question I've been carrying around. Rescriber, a tool that helps people decide what to remove from their prompts before sending them, is the kind of thing I'd want to build: something that gives people real control in the moment they're sharing. Her recent argument that inference and data aggregation get far less research attention than they deserve described my afternoon with two "anonymized" columns better than I could.

I don't know yet exactly what my dissertation will be. I do know which question I want to spend the next five years on: how to help people see what their data reveals before someone else does.
