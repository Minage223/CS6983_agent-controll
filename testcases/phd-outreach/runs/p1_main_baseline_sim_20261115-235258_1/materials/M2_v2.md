**Statement of Interest: PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

---

As a research assistant forecasting bus ridership, I spent an afternoon testing a feature that never made it into our model. I combined two columns from our anonymized data: the stop where a frequent rider usually first boarded, and the area where their phone usually was at night. Neither column seemed revealing alone. Together, they narrowed many riders down to just a few city blocks. The feature didn't improve the model, so I dropped it, and nothing about our project was out of line. But I kept thinking about where the second column came from. The mobility data came from software in partner apps, where people had given one-time consent by tapping "allow." I doubt anyone who tapped it pictured a graduate student, years later, inferring roughly where they sleep. And as far as I know, they had no way to find out it was possible.

Since then, I have kept coming back to two questions. What do people believe can be inferred from the data they share? And could they ever see what is actually inferred? These questions matter even more now that people share far more with AI tools they talk to than they ever did with a transit app. I am applying to the HCI PhD program at Khoury to study them.

**How I got here**

My background is in quantitative work. After a B.A. in Economics, I spent a year as a data analyst at Lakeshore Regional Transit Authority. I maintained ridership dashboards for planners and automated our weekly reporting pipeline. Redesigning a dashboard for board members taught me early that data is only as useful as people's understanding of it.

In my M.S. in Data Science at Lakeshore University, I joined Prof. S. Patel's Urban Analytics Lab. There I built gradient-boosted models on four years of passenger counter data joined with anonymized mobility data, and wrote the first draft of a first-author paper that I presented at the UrbanDS 2025 workshop. That work gave me a practitioner's view of how personal data is used in research. I know which joins are easy, which features get explored and quietly dropped, and how far "anonymized" data is from anonymous once it is combined with other data. The revision process also shaped how I do research. A reviewer suspected that our lagged mobility features leaked future information, so I re-ran the analysis with a longer lag and reported the weaker result alongside the original. I want to bring the same habit of testing my own claims to studying people.

My first real HCI research was a usability study in a Human-Centered Data Science course. My team of three ran think-aloud sessions with five classmates using an AutoML tool. Most participants accepted the top-ranked model without checking its metrics. Two of them assumed the tool "knew" which columns were sensitive and had handled them. It hadn't. These were data science graduate students, and even they held a confident, wrong mental model of what an automated system knew about their data. The study was small and unpublished, but it showed me that the gap I had seen from the data side could be studied directly, by watching how people reason about what a system can see and do.

I have also spent much of my time translating between technical results and the people who act on them. At Northpoint Insurance, I presented a churn model to a VP and the retention team. As a teaching assistant for a statistics course for non-majors, I helped design a rubric that graded interpretation separately from analysis. Both experiences sharpened my interest in how people form beliefs about what data reveals.

**What I want to study**

I am most interested in the gap between what people think they are disclosing and what can be inferred from it. Directions I would like to pursue:

- **Mental models of inference.** When people share location with an app or personal details with a chatbot, what do they believe a system or a determined analyst could infer, especially from combining their data with other sources? Where are those beliefs most wrong, and does it matter for what they choose to share?
- **Making inference visible.** Consent is usually collected once, up front, about data rather than about inferences. I want to explore interfaces that show people what their disclosures make possible, in a form they can act on, and test whether that changes their decisions.
- **Disclosure to AI tools.** Conversational AI invites people to share more context than any form ever asked for. I want to study how people decide what to share with these tools, and how the tools themselves could help people share less without making them less useful.

I know my methods training is weighted toward modeling, and I want to build real depth in qualitative and experimental methods. I think my technical background will help with research on inference, because I have built the kinds of pipelines whose inferences I want to make visible.

**Why Khoury**

I am especially interested in working with **Prof. Tianshi Li** and the PEACH Lab. Her CHI 2024 study of how users navigate disclosure risks with LLM-based chatbots found that people's mental models and the systems' design limited their awareness of privacy risks. That is very close to the question I want to pursue. Rescriber's approach of user-led data minimization is the kind of work I hope to do: tools that give people perceptible control over what they disclose. Her recent position paper with Niloofar Mireshghallah argues that inference attacks and data aggregation are understudied relative to memorization. My afternoon with two "anonymized" columns was a small, concrete instance of exactly that problem. **[Optional: a second faculty member and one sentence on fit.]**

I finish my M.S. in December 2026. I have spent several years on the analyst's side of personal data. I want to spend the next several working on the side of the people the data comes from, helping them understand what their data can reveal before they share it.
