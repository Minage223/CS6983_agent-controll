Last spring, I watched a classmate train a model in an AutoML dashboard, accept the top-ranked result, and move on. She never looked at its metrics. Neither did most of the other participants in our usability study. None of the five opened the feature-importance panel, and two assumed the tool already "knew" which columns were sensitive and had handled them. These were data science graduate students, the people best placed to question the tool. That study changed my question. I had been asking how to build a more accurate model. Now I want to know how people decide what to trust in a model's output, and how tools could support that judgment rather than replace it. I am applying to the HCI PhD program at Khoury to study that question properly.

**How I got here**

I came to this question through the people who use models, not through modeling itself. After a B.A. in Economics, I worked for a year as a data analyst at Lakeshore Regional Transit Authority. I maintained ridership dashboards for the planning department and automated our weekly reporting pipeline. The technical work went well, but the hardest problem was making the numbers usable. When I was asked to adapt a dashboard for board members, I had to rethink what it showed: which comparisons mattered, what to cut, and how to show uncertainty without inviting false precision. A chart I found obvious was not obvious to the people making service decisions from it.

In my M.S. in Data Science at Lakeshore University, I joined Prof. S. Patel's Urban Analytics Lab as a research assistant, building gradient-boosted models to forecast bus ridership recovery. I led the feature engineering and evaluation and wrote the first draft of our paper, which I presented at the UrbanDS 2025 workshop. The revision process taught me more than the modeling did. A reviewer asked whether our lagged mobility features leaked future information. I re-ran the analysis with a longer lag and reported the weaker result alongside the original. I learned that a credible claim depends on anticipating how a skeptical reader will test it. But when I presented the work, the questions I found most interesting were not about MAPE. They were about whether a transit planner would actually change a decision based on a forecast, and what they would need to see first.

I saw the same gap in industry. As a data science intern at Northpoint Insurance, I built a churn model and presented it to the retention team and a VP. Most of my preparation went into explaining what an AUC of 0.76 does and does not mean for deciding who to call. As a teaching assistant for STAT 1100, a statistics course for non-majors, I helped write a grading rubric in which "interpretation" was a separate criterion from "analysis." The most common weak answers came from students who ran the right test and then drew a conclusion the result did not support.

**What I want to study**

Across these settings, the hardest problem was what happened between a model's output and a human decision. I want to study that space empirically and design for it. Directions that interest me:

- **Calibrated reliance on automated data tools.** Our AutoML study was small and unpublished: five participants in a course project. Still, it suggested that users read a tool's polish as competence, including competence it did not have. I would like to study when and why users defer to AutoML and AI-assisted analysis tools, and which interface interventions lead them to check, question, or override the system.
- **Communicating uncertainty to decision-makers.** Planners, managers, and the public routinely act on forecasts and risk scores. I am interested in how forecast uncertainty can be presented so that non-specialists make better decisions with it, rather than ignoring it or being paralyzed by it.

My training is mostly quantitative, and I know I still need to build depth in qualitative and design methods. The think-aloud study was my first real exposure to them, and it is a large part of why I want a PhD rather than another modeling role. I bring experience building, evaluating, and deploying the kinds of models whose users I want to study, and I know firsthand where those models' outputs get misread.
