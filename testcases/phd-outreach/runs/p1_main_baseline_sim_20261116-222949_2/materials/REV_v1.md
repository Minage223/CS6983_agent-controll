**Statement of Purpose — Morgan Reyes, PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

For most of 2025, I worked as a research assistant on a project forecasting bus ridership. Part of our data came from a vendor that collected location data through software built into partner apps. Partway through, I read the vendor's documentation to find out where the data came from. People had agreed to share their location once, by tapping "allow" in some app. Everything we did was anonymized, aggregated, and within the rules. My honest reaction was: I doubt anyone tapping "allow" pictured this.

I couldn't stop thinking about that. Technically, those people had consented. But I wasn't sure what they had actually agreed to, or whether anyone had ever asked them. And nothing in my training as a data scientist helped me answer it. I knew how to join their data to ridership counts, build features from it, and measure how much it improved a forecast. I had no way to find out what it meant to them.

When the paper came out, I mostly felt relieved. My advisor suggested a follow-up extending the model to light rail, and I realized I didn't want another forecasting project. What I wanted to study was the people on the other side of the data.

The first time I got to do that was a course project in Human-Centered Data Science. My team watched five classmates use an AutoML tool and asked them to think aloud. Two of them assumed the tool "knew" which columns in their data were sensitive and would handle them. It didn't. These were data science students, and they were confidently wrong about what a system was doing with their data, in the same way I suspected the app users were. It was a small study, and the first one I had ever run. But sitting with people while they worked taught me more than a year of modeling had, and it's the kind of work I want to keep doing.

Looking back, my earlier jobs pointed in this direction too. As a data analyst at a regional transit agency, I made dashboards. The work I remember is reworking one so board members could read it the way analysts did. As a TA for a statistics course for non-majors, I spent much of my time on how students interpreted results, not on how they calculated them. Both times, the hard part was the gap between what the data said and what people understood.

That gap is what I want to study. My central question is **what people believe happens to their data once they share it, and how consent could be designed so that what people agree to matches what is actually done with their data.** I'm most interested in data that moves out of the setting where it was collected, like location data from an app ending up in a transit agency's planning model. That movement is invisible to the person who shared the data, and a one-time "allow" can't describe it.

Two related questions feed into this one. First, how do people form their beliefs about what a data system knows and does? My classmates' assumptions about the AutoML tool weren't random. They came from somewhere, and consent can't be designed well without understanding them. Second, can the movement of data be made visible? Could people see where their data goes, how it's combined, and what it's used for, in a way they actually understand and that changes the choices they make?

I don't know yet exactly what my dissertation will be, and I want a PhD in part to find out with an advisor's help. I do know I want to answer these questions by talking to and watching people: interviews, think-aloud studies, and fieldwork. I also want to design and test alternatives to the "allow" button, not only document what's wrong with it. Having been the person on the receiving end of this data probably helps. I've seen what happens to it after people agree.
