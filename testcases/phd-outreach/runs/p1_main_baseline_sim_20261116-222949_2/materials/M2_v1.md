**Statement of Purpose — Morgan Reyes, PhD in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

In my Human-Centered Data Science course last spring, my team watched five classmates build models in an AutoML tool while they talked through what they were doing. All five took the top-ranked model without looking at its metrics. None of them opened the feature-importance panel. Two said they assumed the tool "knew" which columns were sensitive and would handle them. These were data science students, the kind of people the tool was made for. It was a small class project, but it changed which questions I care about. Most of my training has been in building models. I want to spend the next five years studying what happens between a model and the people who rely on it: what they think it knows, when they trust it, and what they believe they agreed to when their data went into it.

My path here runs through applied data science. After a B.A. in economics, I worked for a year as a data analyst at Lakeshore Regional Transit Authority. I maintained the ridership dashboards that our planning department used each week, and I automated our weekly reporting pipeline. The part of the job I remember most was not technical. A dashboard that worked fine for analysts confused board members, and I spent a lot of time reworking it until they could read it the way it was meant to be read. I saw that the same number can mean very different things to different audiences. I also saw that people stop trusting a forecast quickly once it has been presented with more confidence than it deserved.

As a research assistant in Prof. S. Patel's Urban Analytics Lab, I led feature engineering and evaluation for a bus ridership forecasting project. The resulting paper, on which I'm first author, appeared at the UrbanDS 2025 workshop. In review, one reviewer argued that our mobility features might leak future information. I re-ran the models with a longer lag. Accuracy dropped slightly but stayed well ahead of the seasonal baseline, and we reported the stricter result. That taught me to design evaluations around the hardest question a skeptic will ask. The project also left me with a question I couldn't answer from inside the model. Our mobility data came from apps whose users had given one-time opt-in consent. Reading the vendor's documentation, I doubted that the people who tapped "allow" had pictured their movements ending up in a transit agency's ridership model. The data was anonymized and aggregated, and the project followed the rules. Even so, I came away wanting to understand the gap between what consent technically covers and what people believe they have agreed to.

This past summer, as a data science intern at Northpoint Insurance, I built a churn model and presented it to the retention team and a VP. Most of my preparation went into explaining AUC and calibrated probabilities to people who would act on the model's scores without seeing how it worked. As a TA for STAT 1100, a statistics course for non-majors, I graded final data projects and wrote a rubric that included examples of strong and weak interpretation. Both jobs showed me how much of a model's effect depends on how people interpret it, which is the part data science training covers least.

These experiences point toward a set of research questions in human-AI interaction:

- **Reliance and overtrust:** How do people decide whether to rely on automated or AI-generated models? What interface designs help them calibrate that trust instead of handing judgment over to the tool?
- **Communicating uncertainty:** How should a forecast's uncertainty be shown to decision-makers, such as planners or managers, who don't have statistical training?
- **Consent and data use:** What do people believe happens to data they share through apps, and how could consent be designed so that it matches the uses their data actually goes to?

I don't yet know which of these will become my dissertation, and I expect my advisor and the first years of coursework to shape that. I do know I want to study these questions with people: through interviews, think-aloud and lab studies, and field work with real decision-makers. I also want to build and test interventions, not only describe problems. My modeling background should help here. I can build the systems I study, run realistic analyses on real data, and recognize when a user's mistaken belief about a model is actually a reasonable reading of a confusing interface.
