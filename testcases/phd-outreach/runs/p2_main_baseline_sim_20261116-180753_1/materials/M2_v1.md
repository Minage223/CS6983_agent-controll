**Statement of Purpose: PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

After Cadence launched AI Coach, I helped code about 1,200 open-ended responses from our in-app survey. AI Coach is a feature that rewrites people's workout plans using their activity, sleep, and heart-rate data. Our coding scheme had three sentiment buckets plus topic tags, and the deck needed one number: 72% positive. One group of responses fit no bucket. These were people saying they didn't understand why their plan had changed. They weren't clearly unhappy, and some kept using the feature. They just couldn't tell what the system had decided, or why. I suggested reporting that theme separately, and it went into the appendix.

I've thought about those responses more than any other result from my four years as a product data analyst. They showed me that my usual measures had answered whether people used the AI. They hadn't answered whether people understood it, or what they believed it was doing with their data. I want to do a PhD in HCI because I want to study that second question properly. In industry it usually ends up in the appendix.

**The question I want to pursue**

More and more consumer AI acts for people using their personal data: it adjusts plans, flags behavior, and makes changes on its own. I'm interested in how people make sense of these changes, and in how designers and researchers can measure that sense-making. Engagement metrics alone can't capture it. My questions are still forming, but they include:

- When an AI system changes something on a person's behalf, what do they think caused it? How does that belief affect whether they accept, override, or quietly ignore the change?
- How does understanding, or the lack of it, show up in behavioral logs? Could log patterns be combined with interviews to detect confusion that engagement numbers hide?
- Does the answer change when the data and the stakes are more personal, as with health or money?

I expect these questions to sharpen once I'm reading the literature seriously and working with an advisor. What I'm sure of is the approach: mixed methods, with qualitative work explaining what people experience and quantitative work showing how widespread it is and what it leads to.

**What I bring: rigor with quantitative evidence**

Since 2022 I have owned the analysis for about 20 A/B tests a year at Cadence, covering onboarding, workouts, and subscriptions. That includes analysis plans, power calculations, and readouts. The work taught me to distrust results that look clean. In one onboarding test I found a 52/48 traffic split where the design called for 50/50. A chi-square test confirmed a sample ratio mismatch (p < 0.001), and we traced it to a client-side redirect that was dropping some Android users from treatment. I recommended re-running the test after the fix rather than reweighting the data, and the PM agreed. In another case, a PM wanted a one-week test on paywall copy. My power calculation showed that detecting a 1-point lift would take about four weeks. I proposed a three-week test with a pre-registered primary metric, and we ran it that way. For ratio metrics like workouts per user, I implemented a user-level bootstrap and checked it against the delta method; the two agreed within 0.2 points.

The AI Coach launch test was my largest analysis: four weeks and about 180,000 users. It showed an 8% increase in weekly workouts (95% CI 6.1–9.9%) and a 2.1-point lift in 7-day retention, with no significant change in subscription conversion. I checked the effect week by week to rule out novelty. I'm proud of that analysis. It's also the clearest example I have of a rigorous answer to an incomplete question.

**Where I am with qualitative research, honestly**

I don't have publications, and my qualitative research experience is mostly from coursework. In my HCI-track undergraduate program, I ran semi-structured interviews with six students and synthesized them through affinity diagramming for a research methods course. For my capstone, my team of four usability-tested a redesigned app prototype with eight students. At Cadence, I volunteered in 2025 to take notes for four user interviews our UX researcher ran on AI Coach. I kept timestamped quotes and separated observations from interpretations so the notes would be easier to synthesize. Watching someone experienced handle those conversations was the most engaging part of my work that year. It also showed me how much of rigorous qualitative research I still need to learn: study design, recruiting, interview technique, and analysis.

That gap is part of why I'm applying for a PhD rather than only pursuing industry research roles. I want formal training in qualitative methods held to the same standard I've learned to apply to experiments. I also want time to study one question in depth instead of one launch at a time.
