**Statement of Purpose: PhD in Human-Computer Interaction, Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

I spend most of my working hours looking at what people do. As a product data analyst at a fitness app, I can tell you how many people opened a feature, how often they came back, and whether a change moved those numbers. I've gotten good at making sure those answers are true. I've become less sure that they're the right answers.

Last year our app launched AI Coach, a feature that rewrites people's workout plans using their sleep, activity, and heart-rate data. By every metric we tracked, it won. Afterward I helped code the open-ended responses from our post-launch survey, and they told a different story. Maybe one in ten said something like *it changed my plan and I have no idea why.* Others asked how it knew they had slept badly. A couple had turned off health-data sharing altogether. In the final deck, all of that became one figure: 72% positive. It felt like the numbers and the people were describing two different products.

I haven't been able to let go of that gap. More and more software uses our own data to make decisions for us: adjusting our plans, flagging our behavior, acting before we ask. Usage can't tell you how someone relates to a system like that. A person can keep using a feature while privately wondering what it knows about them, or quietly start pulling their data back. I want to understand what people think is going on when an app uses their own data to decide things for them, and what would make them feel they still have a say.

The closest I've come to studying this was sitting in on interviews. Last year I volunteered to take notes for a few sessions our UX researcher ran on AI Coach. What stayed with me was hearing people try to explain out loud what the coach was doing: building a theory as they talked, revising it, sometimes contradicting themselves. The researcher mostly let them keep going. I realized that the understanding I couldn't find in our logs was there in the room, but only because someone knew how to leave space for it. It was the most interesting part of my month, and it showed me how much skill that kind of listening takes.

I should be clear about where I'm starting. I have no publications. My research experience so far is two undergraduate projects in my HCI-track degree, an interview study and a usability test, plus what I learned by watching a good researcher work. What I do have is years of practice being careful with evidence: questioning results that look too clean and refusing to report a conclusion the data can't support. That care came to me through numbers. I want to learn it for the kinds of evidence that explain *why*: interviews, observation, and people thinking out loud.

That's why I'm applying for a PhD, and not only for research jobs. In industry, findings like the ones in that survey get noted and moved past, because the next launch is always coming. The question I care about needs room to be studied slowly and from several angles, by someone trained to do it well. I'd like to become that person.
