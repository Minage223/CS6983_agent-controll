**Statement of Purpose: PhD in Human-Computer Interaction, Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

I spend most of my working hours looking at what people do. As a product data analyst at a fitness app, I can tell you how many people opened a feature, how often they came back, and whether a change moved those numbers. I've gotten good at making sure those answers are true. I've become less sure that they're the right answers.

The doubt began with a survey. Our app had launched an AI feature that rewrites people's workout plans based on their sleep, activity, and heart rate. By every measure I was responsible for, it worked. When I read the open-ended responses afterward, though, I kept finding versions of the same sentence: *I don't understand why my plan changed.* These people weren't angry, and many were still using the feature. They had simply accepted a decision made about their body, with their data, without knowing why. Those responses fit none of our categories. To me they were the most interesting thing in the dataset.

I still think about them because they describe something bigger than one feature. More and more software acts for us: adjusting, flagging, deciding. A person's relationship to that software can't be read off their usage. Someone can use a system every day while holding a completely wrong picture of what it's doing, and the behavioral data I work with can't tell the difference. I want to learn how people build their understanding of AI that acts for them, what happens when that understanding is wrong, and how researchers can study this without reducing it to an engagement metric.
