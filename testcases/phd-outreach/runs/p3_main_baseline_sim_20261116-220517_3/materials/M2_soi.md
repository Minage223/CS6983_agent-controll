**Statement of Purpose: PhD in Computer Science (Human-Computer Interaction), Khoury College of Computer Sciences**

Last winter I was transcribing interviews for a study on how teens use online communities for mental-health support. One participant described a server that had been one of the few places they could say they were having a bad day. Then the moderators added an automated filter, and some of their posts quietly disappeared. They understood why the moderators had done it. Still, they described feeling silenced by a tool that was supposed to protect them. That interview has stayed with me, along with the question it raised: who are automated moderation tools actually for? And how do they change what young people feel safe saying when they're looking for help? I don't have a full answer, or even a finished research question. But I've found that this is the kind of problem I keep coming back to, and I'm applying to the HCI PhD program at Khoury to learn how to study it well.

Since October 2025 I've been an undergraduate research assistant in the Social Computing Lab at Brookfield University, working with Prof. A. Moreno and PhD student J. Kim. J. Kim leads an interview study with 16 teens aged 15–17 about how they use online communities for mental-health support. My role was supporting, not leading. I sent recruitment messages to community moderators and ran the eligibility screener. I transcribed and de-identified 14 interviews. Then I coded six transcripts with the team's codebook. In our inter-rater reliability check with J. Kim we reached a Cohen's kappa of 0.71 after one round of reconciliation. The paper is now under review, and I'm listed in the acknowledgments.

The work taught me two things I'd want to build on. First, de-identification meant deciding, line by line, what could identify a teenager: a school, a username, the name of a server. I came away understanding how much trust participants placed in us, and how fragile anonymity can be. Second, coding taught me that disagreement is data. When J. Kim and I coded a passage differently, writing memos to explain our reasoning often showed that the passage was ambiguous in a way the codebook hadn't anticipated.

I'd seen a different side of the same problem a year earlier. In a machine learning course, my team of three fine-tuned a small transformer to classify the sentiment of posts from Reddit mental-health support communities. It reached a macro-F1 of 0.81, against 0.72 for our baseline. My part was the error analysis, and that was the part I found most interesting. The model often labeled sarcastic or dark-humor posts as negative. Some were people joking about a bad week. Others were people saying something serious in a way that only sounded like a joke. At the time it was just an accuracy problem. After the interview, I saw it differently. A moderation filter built on similar signals could remove the very posts where someone is reaching out in the only way they feel comfortable.

That's the direction I hope to explore. When teens know an automated system is watching, how do they decide what to say, how to phrase it, and whether to post at all? Do they soften their language, move to private messages, or leave? And how do moderators weigh protecting a community against making it a place where people can still say they're struggling? I'd like to approach these questions with interviews and qualitative analysis, which I've practiced, alongside computational methods, such as examining what moderation and sentiment models get wrong on support-community language. Eventually I'd like to explore design alternatives that give young people more agency over how their words are handled. I see this partly as a question about disclosure and privacy: when people share something vulnerable, they're making a judgment about who will see it and what will happen next. Automated moderation adds a new audience to that judgment.
