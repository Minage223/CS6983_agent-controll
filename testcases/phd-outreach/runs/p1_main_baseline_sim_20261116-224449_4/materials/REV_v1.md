# Statement of Purpose: Ph.D. in Computer Science (HCI), Khoury College of Computer Sciences

The question that brought me to HCI came up while I was reading a vendor's documentation.

I was a research assistant building models to forecast bus ridership, and part of our data came from a mobility vendor: phone location pings, anonymized and aggregated. I wanted to know where that data came from, so I looked it up. It came from SDKs in partner apps, and people had agreed to it once, by tapping "allow." Everything we did with it was legitimate. Still, I didn't think anyone tapping that button had pictured their movements ending up in a transit forecast. I kept thinking about the gap between what people agree to and what actually happens to their data, a gap that runs through an interface someone designed.

Until then I had thought of myself as someone who builds models. I came to data science from economics, and my first job was as an analyst at a regional transit authority, maintaining ridership dashboards for planners. The part of that job I remember most is redesigning a dashboard for board members, not building the pipeline. The numbers were all correct, but they couldn't use them. The problem had nothing to do with the data. It was about the people reading it.

The ridership research went well. We wrote a workshop paper, and I learned to be rigorous about evaluation. But when my advisor suggested extending the model to light rail, I realized I didn't want to do another forecasting project. The parts that held my interest were the questions around the model: would a planner actually trust this forecast? Should they? Did the people in the data know they were in it?

This spring I finally got to study those questions directly. In a Human-Centered Data Science course, my team ran a small think-aloud study of an AutoML tool with five classmates. All of them accepted the top-ranked model without checking it. No one opened the feature-importance panel. Two assumed the tool already knew which columns were sensitive and would handle them. These were data science students, people like me. Watching them, I understood that much of what goes wrong with these systems happens before the model runs, in what people assume the system is doing for them. It was a class project, and a small one. It was also the first time research felt like the thing I actually wanted to be doing.

I had seen versions of this before without a name for it. As a TA for an intro statistics course for non-majors, I graded final projects where students ran the analysis correctly and then said far more than it could support. In an industry internship, I had to explain a churn model to a team that would act on it. I spent more time on what they would take away than on the model.

I don't have a polished research agenda yet, and I don't want to pretend I do. What I have is a set of questions I keep returning to, all about the people around AI systems rather than the systems themselves:

- How do people decide whether to trust a model's output, and what would help them trust it the right amount?
- What do people assume tools like AutoML are doing for them, and how can an interface correct those assumptions?
- What would consent look like if it matched what actually happens to people's data?

I know my background looks like data science, and in many ways it is. I can bring that technical grounding to HCI research. What I need, and what I'm applying for, is training in qualitative methods and design and in studying people carefully. My one usability study showed me how much I don't know yet, and that I want to learn it.
